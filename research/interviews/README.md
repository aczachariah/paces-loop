# PACES candidate discovery interviews

## Evidence base

Two qualitative interviews were supplied by the product owner on 2026-11-08:

- [Mareen — July 2025, UK trainee, first-attempt pass](mareen-paces-interview.md)
- [Mesh — March 2025, UK trainee, first-attempt pass](mesh-paces-interview.md)

The transcripts preserve the supplied wording. Mareen is the product owner's wife; Mesh is the product owner's sister. Both are doctors with recent, directly relevant PACES experience, but the small, related, UK-trainee, first-attempt-pass sample is not representative of all candidates. Hypothetical willingness to use or pay is not observed behaviour.

## What the participants actually said

### Shared preparation behaviour

- Real-patient examination with a study partner was the most valuable preparation for both.
- They increased deliberate practice over months, including timed/mock encounters.
- Presentation and examiner-style questioning were part of the useful partner activity.
- Both found passive video resources much less useful than active, in-person practice.
- Both said they would have used structured, interactive station practice with feedback.
- Both said they would be open to beta testing a future product.

### Mareen's specific evidence

- Examined patients and practised presenting with a PACES buddy 2–3 times weekly.
- Used the study partner/book to viva each other and checked findings against patients' notes and letters.
- Had already used ChatGPT to provide cases and ask consultation-station questions.
- Explicitly requested a station simulation involving consultation, presentation, viva questions and feedback at the end.
- Wanted help bringing examination findings together into differentials.
- Wanted neurology teaching that explained pathways and lesion localisation before examination practice.
- Stated £50–£100 when asked what would make her pay; said over £200 would be too expensive.

### Mesh's specific evidence

- Prepared for approximately six months, increasing real-patient and mock-station practice near the exam.
- Described the long-term study-partner relationship as essential for learning and progressing together.
- Wanted an interactive website that mimicked study-partner mock scenarios rather than passive video learning.
- Wanted repeatable, useful resources for heart murmurs and chest sounds.
- Identified complexity and price as potential reasons not to use a product.
- Gave an indicative willingness to pay of potentially a couple of hundred pounds, dependent on what was included.

## Product interpretation

These are design hypotheses derived from the interviews, not additional participant statements:

1. **The core product is an available mock-exam partner, not a finding-discovery widget.** A useful encounter must include examination/consultation, spoken synthesis, viva and end-of-station feedback.
2. **Immediate observation feedback belongs in guided practice.** An uninterrupted mock mode should defer corrective feedback until after the encounter, consistent with Mareen's requested sequence.
3. **Spoken presentation and viva are core, not optional polish.** They reproduce the study-partner behaviour both participants valued.
4. **Virtual practice supplements real-patient work.** Neither interview supports replacing ward examination or suggesting that verbal action selection demonstrates hands-on competence.
5. **Neurology localisation and reviewed auscultation material are promising focused expansions.** They should be tested as separate learning activities rather than assumed to belong in the first examination pilot.
6. **Simplicity is part of the value.** The focused workspace is consistent with Mesh's explicit concern about complexity.
7. **£50–£100 is a reasonable initial pricing hypothesis for a focused offer.** It comes primarily from Mareen's answer; Mesh's higher indicative figure was contingent on scope. No pricing decision should be presented as market-validated from these interviews alone.

## First pilot and decision question

Test one clinician-reviewed cardiovascular encounter:

> timed examination → spoken presentation → viva → end-of-station feedback → one targeted retry

Provide a guided mode separately from the uninterrupted mock. Include editable transcripts and typed fallback.

Ask Mareen and Mesh to test separately using real microphone input. The decision question is:

> Does this reproduce enough of the useful study-partner experience that they would choose it between bedside sessions?

Observe completion without facilitator rescue, speech/action-routing failures, feedback accuracy, whether the retry addresses the actual weakness and whether the encounter feels usefully exam-like. Do not use satisfaction alone as evidence of clinical competence, examination readiness or improved pass rates.

## Follow-up research

- Test the same workflow with unrelated current/recent candidates, including IMGs and candidates who have failed an attempt.
- Ask about behaviour and trade-offs rather than only hypothetical interest.
- Test pricing with an actual scope and purchase/deposit decision when appropriate.
- Keep candidate usability evidence separate from qualified clinician review of case content and feedback.
